## Study design fixes before submission

1. **Move the post-test before the interpretation interview.** Interview discussion can cue meanings. Proposed sequence: consent/background → pre-test → unprompted encounter and free exploration → immediate post-test → interpretation interview → debrief.
2. **Acknowledge pre-test priming.** Even without translations, a pre-test directs attention toward characters and meaning. With faculty, choose either this exploratory pre/post design with an explicit limitation, or drop recognition testing and prioritize natural discovery. Avoid dividing a small sample into many conditions.
3. **Measure immediate recognition accurately.** An immediate post-test does not establish long-term retention. Use “immediate meaning recognition” in the paper. A delayed test is an optional protocol addition, not necessary scope for this submission.
4. **Define exposure.** A display event is not proof someone noticed a character. Distinguish displayed, activated and observed-attended events. Choose a defensible primary rule before analysis and report its limits.
5. **Keep discovery separate from coverage.** Record unprompted discovery first. If a later guided phase ensures selected characters are encountered, label it separately and specify it in the protocol.
6. **Define assistance.** Choose a consistent wait interval and neutral assistance script. Record whether discovery occurred before assistance.
7. **Avoid causal claims from categories.** Individual characters, animation quality and familiarity differ; category comparisons should be descriptive unless the design supports stronger inference.

Suggested 35-minute session: 4 minutes consent/background; 3 minutes pre-test; 15 minutes discovery/exploration; 3 minutes post-test; 8 minutes interview; 2 minutes debrief. This retains Dave's suggested exploration duration while allowing the other tasks. Validate timing in the approved pilot. The earlier 15–20-minute estimate for the entire session is too tight for all proposed stages.

### During collection

After every session: export/verify logs, secure consent and recordings, write an observation memo, record deviations and start transcription/coding. Schedule four to five sessions per week across the main window, with additional slots as needed. Two to three pilot participants are separate unless inclusion is justified in advance and the final procedure is comparable.

### Analysis deliverables

- RQ1: an evidence table connecting interpretation patterns, participant quotations, character examples and counterexamples. Document the qualitative approach and how interpretations were reviewed.
- RQ2: discovery counts with denominators, median time to interaction, assistance, failed touches and descriptive movement/interaction patterns. Report cases with no discovery within the observation window separately.
- RQ3: participant-level summaries for previously unknown, qualifying-exposure characters; show numerators and denominators, accepted meaning variants, missing responses and exclusions. Have Hiroko help define a scoring rubric. Do not treat repeated character responses from one participant as independent people.
- State prior familiarity, pre-test priming, exposure uncertainty, convenience sampling, short sessions and the absence of a causal control as relevant limitations.
- Save analysis scripts or calculation notes so numbers can be checked against raw records.

### Proposed paper structure

1. Motivation and focused contribution.
2. Closest related work and positioning.
3. Installation and design rationale, with a character-to-transformation figure.
4. Study method, ethics context and analysis approach.
5. Findings supported by observations, quotes and modest descriptive statistics.
6. Design implications, limitations and discussion questions for CHI attendees.

Write the motivation, related work and design sections during October. Add findings only after analysis. For every headline claim, identify the specific evidence supporting it.
